Does Shakespeare Say "Thou"? A Cheat Sheet to the Thou/You Code

Yes. Shakespeare says thou all the time. His characters use thou and you the way we use first names and titles. One word means closeness or contempt.

The other means respect or distance. If you want to hear the difference in your own sentences, try a Shakespearean English translator and listen to how thou changes the tone of a plain line.

Thou is not just an old word for you. It is a social signal. A king uses thou to a servant. A lover uses thou to a lover.

A man uses thou to an enemy he wants to insult. The same word can warm a line or wound with it. This guide shows the code, the table, and five moments where the switch changes the scene.

The pronoun table

Start with the forms. Each one has a job. Mix them up and the line sounds wrong. The table below covers the forms you will meet most in the plays.

FormUseExample
thousubject, one personThou art kind.
theeobject, one personI love thee.
thypossessive before a consonantthy heart
thinepossessive before a vowel, or alonethine eyes
yesubject, a group, or formal singularYe are welcome.
youobject, a group, or formal singularI thank you.
yourpossessive, a group or formalyour grace
yourspossessive pronounThe choice is yours.
thyselfreflexiveTake heed of thyself.

Verbs change with thou too. Thou takes an -est ending, and third person singular takes -eth.

  • thou thinkest, thou speakest
  • he runneth, she speaketh
  • art means are
  • hast means have
  • dost means do
  • hath means has
  • doth means does

Thine follows the same rule as mine. Use thine before a vowel sound and thy before a consonant.

Say thine eyes but thy heart. Say thine honour but thy sword. The rule is old, and it still works.

The word has a long history. Thou began as the plain singular, then took on the social code in the 1300s, then faded from standard speech. You can read that history on the Wikipedia page for thou.

Four rules for thou and you

The whole code rests on four rules. Learn them and you can read the pronoun in any scene.

1. Rank flows down

A king says thou to a servant. A master says thou to a worker. The servant answers with you. The worker answers with you.

Thou moves down the ladder. You moves up it.

2. Love speaks thou

Lovers use thou when they are close. Juliet says thou to Romeo in the balcony scene. The sonnets speak to the beloved with thee. Shall I compare thee to a summer's day.

That thee is warm, not rude. It is the sound of two people who trust each other.

3. Anger speaks thou too

Thou is also a weapon. Kent calls Oswald a boil and a plague sore with thou. Macbeth calls a servant a cream-faced loon.

The pronoun is the same as the lovers use. The tone makes it an insult. Rank and rage pull the same word in opposite ways.

4. You marks respect and distance

Strangers get you. Superiors get you. A whole group gets ye or you. When in doubt, use you.

It is the safe choice in a world that ran on rank. Too much thou can offend. Too much you only sounds stiff.

Who gets thou? A quick guide

  • A close friend or a lover: thou
  • A child, a servant, or a follower: thou
  • A stranger, or someone above you: you
  • A king, a lord, or a judge: you
  • A group of people: ye, or you
  • Someone you mean to insult: thou

The pattern has a name. Linguists call it the T-V distinction, from the Latin tu and vos. The scholar David Crystal sums it up in plain terms. You goes up the ladder, and thou comes down it.

You also marks a change in feeling. A sudden switch of pronoun is a sudden switch of mood. The T-V distinction was still strong when Shakespeare began to write, and it faded from standard English in the first half of the 17th century.

Five switches that show the code at work

The code is clearest when a character switches in mid-scene. Here are five switches. Each one changes the meaning of the line. Read the quote, then read the note.

1. The Nurse and Juliet

"Thou wast the prettiest babe that e'er I nursed."

The Nurse to Juliet, Romeo and Juliet, Act 1, Scene 3

"Madam, I am here. What is your will?"

Juliet to the Nurse, Romeo and Juliet, Act 1, Scene 3

The Nurse raised Juliet, and she speaks to her with thou. Juliet answers with you. The Nurse is below Juliet in rank, yet she uses thou. The word here is affection, not rank.

Juliet's you is respect in return. One scene holds both directions at once.

2. The balcony scene

"Wherefore art thou Romeo."

Juliet, Romeo and Juliet, Act 2, Scene 2

Juliet speaks to Romeo with thou in the balcony scene. The word is part of the love scene. Only thou fits the moment.

The pair are close, young, and alone. A formal word would break the spell.

3. Hal and Falstaff

"God save thee, my sweet boy!"

Falstaff to Hal, Henry IV, Part 2, Act 5, Scene 5

"I know thee not, old man."

Hal, now King Henry V, to Falstaff, Henry IV, Part 2, Act 5, Scene 5

Falstaff greets the new king with the old affection. Hal answers with the same word and the opposite meaning. Falstaff's thee is love. Hal's thee is a king speaking down.

The pronoun stays put while the friendship ends. The Folger Shakespeare Library gives the backstory of Falstaff and the king who cuts him off. SparkNotes walks through the scene line by line.

4. Kent and Oswald

"Thou art a boil, a plague sore, an embossed carbuncle in my corrupted blood."

"Thou art a base, proud, shallow, beggarly, three-suited, hundred-pound, filthy worsted-stocking knave."

Kent to Oswald, King Lear, Act 2, Scene 2

Kent meets Oswald and drops thou like a slap. He goes on in the same scene, and the second blow lands harder.

Kent is a lord in disguise as a servant. Oswald is a steward. The thou puts Oswald in his place.

5. Sir Toby and Malvolio

"Dost thou think, because thou art virtuous, there shall be no more cakes and ale?"

Sir Toby Belch to Malvolio, Twelfth Night, Act 2, Scene 3

Sir Toby teases Malvolio with thou. The thou is mockery. Malvolio is a steward who acts above his station.

Toby uses thou to cut him down to size. The joke lands because the pronoun does the work.

One play runs the whole code. Hamlet greets Horatio with you in Act 1, Scene 2. Horatio, I am glad to see you well.

By Act 3, Scene 2, the friendship has deepened and the pronoun has changed. Horatio, thou art e'en as just a man. Hamlet keeps you for Claudius, his king, and thou for the Ghost in Act 1, Scene 4. Be thou a spirit of health or goblin damn'd.

Three people, two pronouns, one rule. The pronoun tells you the relationship before the plot does.

Three mistakes everyone makes

Three traps catch almost everyone who tries this. Here they are, with the fix for each.

Thou is not formal

Many people think thou sounds grand and formal. It is the opposite. Thou is warm, low, or rude. You is the formal word.

If you call a king thou, you insult him. If you call a friend you, you cool the friendship. The grand feel comes from church and old books, not from the plays.

Thee is not a fancy you

Thee is the object form. I love thee is right. Thee loves me is wrong. Thou is the subject.

Thee takes the action. Keep them apart and the grammar holds. Swap them and the line falls apart.

Ye is not a typo

Ye is the old plural subject. Ye are welcome means all of you are welcome. You took over both jobs in time. Ye fell away, and you stayed.

Do not swap them by ear. Read ye as a group, and the line makes sense.

Quick answers

Does every Shakespeare play use thou?

Almost every play uses thou. The history plays and tragedies use it most. The late plays use it less. The word fades as the plays go on, and it dies out of common speech in the first half of the 17th century.

Did people in Shakespeare's time talk like this?

Yes, in broad terms. Thou and you were part of daily speech in London. The plays heighten the code for drama.

Real speech was messier, and people broke the rules. The stage keeps the rules clean so the audience can read them.

When did thou die out?

Thou fades from standard English in the first half of the 17th century. It lives on in some northern dialects and in prayer. Modern speakers meet it in the King James Bible and in wedding vows. Shakespeare catches it at its peak, just before the fall.

Why does thou sound formal now?

Because we meet it in church and in old books. The old use was warm or rude, not grand. The solemn feel is a later habit.

Shakespeare's audience heard it as everyday speech. We hear it as old speech, and the gap does the rest.

Thou is a small word with a large job. It marks love, rank, and rage in four letters. Learn the code and the plays open up.
