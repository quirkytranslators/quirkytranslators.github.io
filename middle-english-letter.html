How to Write a Letter in Middle English

Written by Arlo Bennett

You write a letter in Middle English by swapping modern greetings and verbs for their medieval forms, then keeping the shape of a normal letter. Start with a warm greeting like "Hail" or "Blisful morwe", use "thee" and "thou" for a close friend and "yow" for a formal reader, and close with thanks. A Middle English Translator turns each modern line into period wording in seconds, so you can build the whole letter line by line.

The trick is not to fake old spelling at random. Middle English had real rules, and readers spot fake "ye olde" spelling at once. Pick one voice for the whole letter, formal or warm, and stay with it to the last line.

Greet your reader the medieval way

Every medieval letter opens with a greeting, and the greeting sets the tone for all that follows. A warm letter to a friend opens with "Blisful morwe, my freend", which means "Good morning, my friend". A lively letter opens with "Hail, how farestow this day?", which means "Hello, how are you today?".

Pick the greeting that fits your reader. Use "freend" for someone close to you, and keep "Hail" for a bright, bold opening. Never mix a warm greeting with a stiff formal body. Your reader will feel the shift, and the letter will read as two letters in one.

Middle English is not Old English

Many writers mix the two periods up, and the mix shows. Old English is the language of Beowulf, spoken before the Norman Conquest of 1066. Middle English came after that conquest, shaped by French words and simpler grammar. If your letter needs the older tongue, use the Old English Translator instead.

Keep the shape of a real letter

A medieval letter has the same bones as a modern one. It opens with a greeting, gives news in the body, and ends with thanks or love. The Paston family letters show this shape in real life. The Pastons wrote to each other about land, money, and family news between about 1422 and 1509, and their letters still read as letters, not poems.

Chaucer gives the period its best-known voice. His Canterbury Tales opens with a spring morning, and the first line is a fine model of the sound. "Whan that Aprill with his shoures soote" means "When April with its sweet showers". Borrow the sound, not the whole line, so your letter reads like you writing five hundred years ago.

Build the body line by line

Write your body in plain modern English first. Keep each line short, one thought per line, so the old wording stays clear. Then turn each line into Middle English with the Middle English Translator, and read the result aloud. If a line sounds stiff, rewrite the modern line and try again.

Short lines work best for news and travel. "The knyght rood to the castel" means "The knight rode to the castle".

"Where is the nexte taverne?" means "Where is the nearest inn?". "What tyme is it now?" means "What time is it now?".

A line about people works the same way. "The kyng spak to his peple" means "The king spoke to his people". One line, one picture, and the reader follows you with no effort. Stack five or six such lines, and you have a body that moves.

What news fits a medieval letter

News of travel suits this form well. A knight on the road, a visit to a castle, and a night at an inn all fit the voice. News of the king and his people fits too, since such news filled real letters of the time. Keep the news to what you saw and heard, and leave out what you only guess.

Ask one plain question in the body. "Where is the nexte taverne?" and "What tyme is it now?" both come from real needs on the road.

A question gives your reader a reason to write back. A letter with no question often gets no reply.

Close with thanks or love

End the letter the way a medieval writer would. "I thank yow for your curteis helpe." means "Thank you for your kind help".

"I love thee with al my hert." means "I love you with all my heart". Choose thanks for a formal letter and love for a close one.

Mind the small words at the close. "Thee" speaks to one loved reader, while "yow" speaks to a reader you hold in respect. Swap them and the tone of the whole letter shifts. Read the closing aloud before you seal the letter.

Check your letter backwards

A good test is to run the old lines back through the tool. "Hail, how farestow this day?" comes back as "Hello, how are you doing today?"

"Blisful morwe, my freend" comes back as "Blessed morning, my friend". The sense holds in both directions, and that is how you know the letter is sound.

If a line comes back with the wrong sense, fix the modern line first. The tool follows your words, so a muddled modern line gives a muddled old line. Clear input, clear output, and the letter stays true.

Mistakes that give a letter away

Three slips mark a letter as fake. First, writers sprinkle "ye" and "thou" at random, where real letters use them with care. Second, they spell every word with extra letters, which no scribe did. Third, they mix Old English words into a Middle English letter.

Keep your spelling steady from the first line to the last. If you write "knyght" once, do not write "knight" later in the same letter. If you write "castel", keep "castel" through the close. A reader trusts a steady hand, and a steady hand is easy to keep.

Seal it and send it

A letter is done when it reads aloud with ease. Read it once for sense, once for sound, and once for spelling. Then fold the page, press a wax seal, and send it on its way. Your reader gets a small piece of the fourteenth century in the post.
