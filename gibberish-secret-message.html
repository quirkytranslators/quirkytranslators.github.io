How to Write a Secret Message in Gibberish

Written by Arlo Bennett

You can write a secret message in gibberish in under five minutes. Type your plain English into the Gibberish Translator, copy the result, and send it to a friend who knows the trick. Anyone else who reads it will see a wall of strange syllables.

The trick is simple. Gibberish hides each word by adding sounds inside it. Your friend reads the coded line, strips those extra sounds away, and finds your real words underneath. This guide shows you the pattern, gives you phrases to practise with, and ends with a full secret message you can copy and send today.

What Gibberish Actually Is

Gibberish is a word game, not a real language. Players take normal English words and stretch them with extra syllables. The words look wild on the page. They still follow a rule, so a friend who knows the rule can read them.

Kids have played versions of this game for years. They pass notes in class, whisper coded lines at lunch, and dare each other to read a whole sentence out loud. The game works because the code is easy to learn and hard to crack by accident.

The version on this page uses the sound "idig" tucked inside words. A short word like "love" becomes "lidigove". A longer word like "morning" becomes "midigornidiging". The extra sounds pile up fast, and that pile is what makes the message look secret.

The One Rule Behind the Code

Every gibberish word starts from a plain English word. The translator slips "idig" sounds into the word after its vowels. The shape of the word stays close enough to spot once you know what to look for.

Look at a simple pair. "I love you" becomes "Idigi lidigove yidigou". Say it out loud and you can hear the original words hiding inside.

"Love" sits in the middle of "lidigove". "You" sits inside "yidigou".

Longer words stretch further. "Good morning, my friend" becomes "Gidigood midigornidiging, myidigy fridigend". The sentence is twice as long as the original.

That extra length is the whole point. It slows down any reader who does not know the rule.

The widget is not always neat about it. "I am going to the store" comes out as "IdigI amidigo goidigoing tidigo thide storeidige". Notice "IdigI" at the start.

The "idig" lands in a odd spot, and the capital I stays at the end. Quote the output exactly as it comes out. Do not tidy it up. Your friend decodes what you sent, not what you meant to send.

Your Five Step Method

Follow these five steps and your first secret message will be ready in minutes.

Step 1: Write the plain message first. Keep it short. Ten to twenty words is plenty for a first try.

Short messages are easier to write and easier to decode. You can go longer once you and your friend get fast at it.

Step 2: Run it through the translator. Paste one sentence at a time. Check each line before you move on. A single wrong word can confuse your friend later.

Step 3: Read the coded version out loud. If you can still hear your words inside it, the code is working. If a line sounds like pure noise, split the sentence into shorter pieces and try again.

Step 4: Send the coded version only. Do not send the plain version in the same message. Do not add a hint that gives the game away. The fun is in the decoding.

Step 5: Agree on a reply code. Ask your friend to answer in gibberish too. A one line reply proves they cracked it. It also keeps the game going.

Five Phrases to Learn First

Learn these five lines and you can already hold a short coded chat. Each one comes from the translator, quoted exactly as it produced it.

Hello: "Hididigellidigo, hididigow aridige yidigou tidigodayidige?" That is the coded form of "Hello, how are you today?" It is a perfect opener for a secret note.

Good morning: "Gidigood midigornidiging, myidigy fridigend" means "Good morning, my friend". Use it to start a message that feels warm, not sneaky.

A kind line: "Idigi lidigove yidigou" means "I love you". Three small words turn into a line that looks far more complex than it is.

A question: "Whidigat idigis yodigour nidigame?" means "What is your name?" Questions work well in code because the question mark gives your friend a clue about the sentence type.

A plain fact: "Thidigeidigo cidigat sidigat onidig thidigeidigo midigat" means "The cat sat on the mat". It is a dull sentence in plain English. In gibberish it looks like a riddle.

Practise these five out loud before you write your own message. Say the plain line, then say the coded line. Your ear learns the pattern faster than your eye does.

How to Build a Longer Message

Short phrases are easy. A full note needs a little planning. Pick one idea per sentence.

Keep each sentence under ten words in plain English. Long coded sentences turn into walls of text that tire your reader out.

Write your note in three parts. Open with a greeting. Put your real news in the middle.

Close with a sign off. Three parts give your friend a clear shape to follow, even when every word looks strange.

Test each part on its own. Translate the greeting first and read it back. Then do the middle lines one by one.

Then do the sign off. If one line looks wrong, fix that line alone and leave the rest in place.

Keep a copy of the plain version for yourself. You will need it if your friend gets stuck on one line. You can then give them a single word as a hint instead of handing over the whole answer.

How Your Friend Decodes It

Decoding is the reverse of encoding. Your friend takes the coded line and works out the plain words inside it. The translator can do this step too. Paste a coded line in and it returns the plain English.

Try it with the greeting. Feed "Hididigellidigo, hididigow aridige yidigou tidigodayidige?" back into the tool and it returns "Hello, how are you today?"

The round trip is exact. What went in comes back out.

The second line works the same way. "Gidigood midigornidiging, myidigy fridigend" decodes to "Good morning, my friend". Your friend can check their own decoding against the tool. If their guess matches the tool, they cracked it.

Teach your friend this check before you send a long note. Send one line first and ask them to decode it. Once they get one line right, they have the pattern. Then send the full message with confidence.

Three Mistakes That Give the Game Away

Mistake 1: Sending the plain text too. Never paste the original under the coded version. One peek ruins the puzzle. If your friend gets stuck, give one word as a hint, not the full answer.

Mistake 2: Making the message too long. A fifty word note becomes a huge block of coded text. Your friend will give up halfway.

Keep your first notes under twenty words. Add length only after your friend decodes one cleanly.

Mistake 3: Fixing the odd outputs. The translator sometimes puts "idig" in a strange place. "IdigI" looks like a typo, but it is the real output.

If you "fix" it, your friend cannot match it to the tool. Quote every line exactly as produced.

Where to Use Secret Messages

Secret notes are made for birthdays. Slip a coded line into a birthday card and let the birthday star decode it at the party. It takes a minute and it gets a laugh.

They work in the classroom too. A coded note passed between friends feels like a small adventure. Keep it kind and keep it short. Teachers tend to smile at a puzzle and frown at a disruption.

Use them in group chats for party plans. A coded line about a surprise plan stays hidden from the guest of honour if they are in the chat and do not know the code. Just make sure at least one other person in the chat knows how to read it.

They also work as a writing game. Give two friends the same plain sentence and compare their coded versions. The outputs should match if both used the same tool. If they differ, one of them typed the sentence in a different way.

A Short History of Coded Play

Coded speech games are old. Children in many countries have played versions where extra syllables get tucked into words. The rules change from place to place, but the idea stays the same. Hide the word in plain sight by stretching it.

Pig Latin is the best known cousin of gibberish. It moves sounds to the end of a word instead of adding sounds inside it.

The habit survives because it is social. A code needs at least two people. One writes, one decodes, and both share the joke.

That is why a short note beats a long essay. The fun is in the exchange, not the length.
